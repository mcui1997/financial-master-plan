## Defense wins

You can do everything right with your investments and still lose it all to one bad event. A disability that ends your income. A death that leaves your family stuck. A lawsuit. This chapter is short, but skipping it is one of the most common gaps in people's plans.

The idea: **insure against disasters, not inconveniences.** Your emergency fund handles the small stuff.

## Health insurance

The single most important one. A serious illness without insurance can cost more than most people will ever save. If you are between jobs, look at COBRA or the marketplace at healthcare.gov. Don't go without.

## Disability insurance

Your ability to earn is likely your biggest asset. Far bigger than your investments, especially when you are young. If an injury or illness kept you from working for years, how would you pay the bills?

- Many employers offer it. Check your benefits for "short-term" and "long-term disability."
- Long-term disability is the one that matters most.
- If your job doesn't offer it, you can buy it on your own.

## Life insurance

You need it **if someone depends on your income**: a spouse, kids, or a parent you support. If no one does, you may not need it at all.

- **Term life** is what most people need. It covers you for a set number of years, like 20 or 30, and is usually inexpensive.
- **Whole life** and other "permanent" policies mix insurance with investing. They are usually far more expensive and are often sold with high commissions. Most people do better with term life plus investing the difference.
- A common rule of thumb is about 10 to 12 times your yearly income. Enough to pay off debts and replace your income while your kids grow up.

## Liability and property

- Keep good home or renters insurance, and car insurance.
- As your net worth grows, consider an **umbrella policy**. It adds extra liability coverage, often $1 million, for a modest yearly cost.

## Beneficiaries: the 5-minute task

Every retirement account, HSA, and life insurance policy lets you name a [[beneficiary]]. That is who gets the money if you die.

**This overrides your will.** If your ex is still listed on an old 401(k), they get it, no matter what your will says.

Log in to each account and check. It takes five minutes. Do it again after any big life change: marriage, divorce, a new child.

## A will and a few documents

You don't need to be wealthy to need these:

- **A will:** who gets your things and who cares for your kids.
- **Power of attorney:** who handles your money if you can't.
- **Health care directive:** who makes medical decisions for you, and what you want.

Simple versions are affordable. Online services or a local attorney can help.

## The "if something happens" note

Write one page for the person who would handle things if you couldn't. List:

- every account and where it is (bank, brokerage, 401(k), HSA, insurance)
- how to reach your employer's benefits office
- where your will and documents are
- who to call for help

Store it somewhere safe and tell that person where it is. Use a password manager with an emergency access feature, or keep instructions with your documents. **Never write passwords on a note you leave lying around.**

This is one of the kindest things you can do for your family.
