## Why safety comes before investing

Investing works over years. Life happens in days. A car breaks down. A job ends. A pipe bursts.

If you have no cash, you have three bad options: a credit card at 25% interest, a loan, or selling your investments, often at the worst time. Safety first is not cautious. It is what lets the rest of the plan survive.

## Step 1: a small starter cushion

Before anything else, save **one month of expenses**. If that feels far off, start with $1,000.

This covers most small surprises. It also breaks the cycle of putting every emergency on a credit card.

## Step 2: clear expensive debt

Debt has an interest rate, called the [[APR|apr]]. Paying off debt is like earning that rate, guaranteed. Use these lines as a guide:

- **Above about 8%:** pay it off before investing beyond your employer match. Credit cards (often 20% to 30%) are the classic example. Nothing you can invest in reliably beats that.
- **About 4% to 8%:** a judgment call. Many people split extra money between paying it down and investing.
- **Below about 4%:** pay it on schedule. Investing likely does better over time. Many older mortgages and some student loans fall here.

Two ways to attack several debts:

- **Avalanche:** pay extra on the highest interest rate first. Saves the most money.
- **Snowball:** pay extra on the smallest balance first. Quick wins keep you going.

Both work. The best one is the one you finish.

## Step 3: a full emergency fund

Once the expensive debt is gone, grow your cushion to **3 to 6 months of must-pay costs**. Use your fixed costs plus basic food and gas, not your full spending. In a real emergency, you would cut the extras.

How many months? Lean toward more if:

- you are the only income in your household
- your income changes month to month, or you work on commission
- your job is in an industry with frequent layoffs
- you have kids, a house, or health issues

## Where to keep it

In a [[high-yield savings account|hysa]], separate from your everyday checking account. Separate means you won't spend it by accident. High-yield means it earns some interest while it waits.

**Not** in stocks or Bitcoin. Emergencies often show up together with bad markets. A recession can bring a layoff and a 30% drop at the same time. Your emergency money has to be whole when you need it.

## When you use it

That is what it is for. Don't feel guilty. Use it, then pause extra investing and refill it. Then go back to the plan.

## Money for planned big costs

An emergency fund is for surprises. Things you know are coming, like a car, a wedding, or a house down payment, get their own savings. Any money you need **within about 5 years** stays in cash or [[T-bills|t-bill]], not in the investment mix.
