## Where your money lives matters

You don't just "invest." You invest **inside an account**. The account decides how your money is taxed. The same fund can leave you with very different amounts depending on where it sits.

Here are the main accounts, in plain words.

## The accounts

**[[401(k)|401k]]** (or 403(b), 457, TSP for some jobs). A retirement account through your employer. Money comes straight out of your paycheck. Many employers add an [[employer match]], which is free money. You pick from a short menu of funds. In {{year}} you can put in up to {{limit:k401}}, plus {{limit:k401CatchUp50}} more if you are 50 or older.

**[[HSA|hsa]]** (Health Savings Account). Only available with a [[high-deductible health plan|hdhp]]. It has the best tax deal of any account: money goes in tax-free, grows tax-free, and comes out tax-free for medical costs. After 65 you can use it for anything, taxed like a traditional IRA. In {{year}}: {{limit:hsaSelf}} for yourself or {{limit:hsaFamily}} for a family plan.

**[[IRA|ira]]** (Individual Retirement Account). An account you open yourself at any brokerage. You can buy almost any fund. In {{year}} you can put in up to {{limit:ira}}, plus {{limit:iraCatchUp50}} if you are 50 or older.

**[[Taxable brokerage account|taxable-account]].** A regular investment account. No tax breaks and no limits. You can take money out any time. You pay tax on dividends each year and on gains when you sell.

## Roth or traditional?

Both 401(k)s and IRAs usually come in two flavors. The only difference is **when** you pay tax.

- **[[Roth|roth]]:** pay tax now. The money grows and comes out tax-free in retirement.
- **[[Traditional|traditional]]:** skip tax now. You pay tax when you take it out in retirement.

A simple rule of thumb:

- **Lower income now, or early in your career:** Roth is usually better. Your tax rate today is likely lower than it will be later.
- **High income now:** traditional often wins. You save tax at your top rate today.
- **Not sure:** splitting between both is a perfectly good answer.

Note: you can't put money into a Roth IRA directly if your income is too high. In {{year}}, the limit starts phasing in at $153,000 for single filers and $242,000 for married couples filing together. See the chapter "When this site isn't enough" if that is you.

## The order to fill them

This is the order for every extra dollar. Do step 1, then step 2, and so on. Most people are somewhere in the middle, and that is fine.

1. **Know your numbers.** You need to know what you can save.
2. **Starter emergency fund.** One month of expenses.
3. **401(k) up to the full match.** It is an instant 50% to 100% return.
4. **Pay off high-interest debt.** Anything above about 8%.
5. **Full emergency fund.** 3 to 6 months.
6. **HSA,** if you have one. The triple tax break is hard to beat.
7. **IRA.** Roth or traditional, up to the limit.
8. **Max your 401(k).** Up to the yearly limit.
9. **Taxable brokerage account.** Everything beyond this point.

## Good to know

- **Retirement accounts lock money up, mostly.** Taking money out of a 401(k) or traditional IRA before age 59½ usually costs a 10% penalty plus tax. There are exceptions. A Roth IRA is more flexible: you can always take out what you **put in** (not the growth) with no tax or penalty.
- **Matches can have strings.** Some employers make you stay a few years before the match is fully yours. That is called [[vesting]].
- **HSA trick.** If you can afford it, pay medical bills from your regular cash and let the HSA keep growing. Save your receipts. You can pay yourself back years later, tax-free.
- **Accounts are boxes, not investments.** Opening an account does not invest your money. You still have to buy funds inside it. More on that in "Buying."
