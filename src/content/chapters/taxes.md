## Why taxes matter for investors

Taxes are one of the biggest costs in investing. Usually bigger than fees. You can't avoid them, but a few simple habits can keep a lot more money working for you.

You don't need to become a tax expert. You need five ideas.

## 1. Tax brackets: only the top slice

The US taxes income in layers, called brackets. The first chunk of income is taxed at a low rate, the next chunk at a higher rate, and so on.

When a raise moves you into a higher bracket, **only the dollars above the line** are taxed at the higher rate. Everything below stays the same. A raise never lowers your take-home pay because of brackets.

Your **[[marginal tax rate]]** is the rate on your last dollar. It is the one that matters when you choose Roth vs. traditional. A traditional 401(k) saves you tax at this rate today.

## 2. Two kinds of investment gains

When you sell something for more than you paid, the profit is a [[capital gain|capital-gains]]. How it is taxed depends on how long you held it:

- **Held one year or less:** taxed like regular income. Often 22% or more.
- **Held more than one year:** taxed at lower long-term rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%, based on your income. Many people pay 15%. Some pay 0%.

**Takeaway:** in a taxable account, try not to sell anything you have held for less than a year.

None of this applies inside a 401(k), IRA, or HSA. There, trades are not taxed at all.

## 3. Dividends and interest

- Most stock fund dividends are "qualified" and taxed at the lower long-term rates.
- Bond interest is taxed like regular income. That makes bonds a bit costly to hold in a taxable account.
- Interest on US Treasury bonds and T-bills is free from state income tax.

## 4. Put things in the right box

This is called asset location. When you have both retirement and taxable accounts:

- **Bonds and REITs** throw off income that is taxed at higher rates. Hold them in a 401(k) or IRA when you can.
- **Stock index funds** are fairly tax-efficient. They are fine in a taxable account.
- **The things you expect to grow the most** do great in a Roth, where growth is never taxed.

This is a "nice to have." If it makes things complicated, skip it. Getting the mix right matters far more.

## 5. Turning losses into a tax break

If something in a taxable account is worth less than you paid, you can sell it and use the loss to cut your taxes. This is called [[tax-loss harvesting]].

- Losses first cancel out gains from other sales.
- Up to $3,000 more a year can reduce your regular income.
- Anything left over carries forward to future years.

Then buy something **similar but not identical**, so your mix stays the same. For example, sell one total stock market fund and buy another company's version.

**Watch the [[wash sale]] rule.** Don't buy the same or a nearly identical investment within 30 days before or after the sale. That applies to **any** account, including your IRA or your spouse's. If you do, you lose the tax break.

## A few last notes

- **Keep records.** Your brokerage tracks what you paid. Keep the yearly tax forms they send (the 1099s).
- **Selling gains to rebalance is OK sometimes.** Paying some tax to keep your risk under control is better than letting one asset take over.
- **Tax rules change.** This chapter covers the big ideas, which change rarely. Rates and limits do change. Check current numbers each year.
- **Complicated situation?** A tax professional can pay for themselves quickly. More on that in "When this site isn't enough."
